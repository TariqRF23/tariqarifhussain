Zetes CRM Automation and Optimization - A Scenario Analysis

Leads arrived on business cards and at trade shows, and somebody typed them into a spreadsheet to get them into Salesforce. This was my first placement, and it ended in five deliverables rather than one: a way of choosing a tool, a way of entering data, a specification for building the thing properly, a business case, and a prototype that actually ran.

The company

Zetes identifies things and people: goods through a supply chain, and people through biometrics and smart cards. It works in more than twenty countries from a head office in Brussels, across manufacturing, pharmaceuticals, warehousing, food and drink, retail, transport, automotive and the postal services, and for governments too.

The case studies in the report give the flavour better than the sector list does. Scanning systems in the forklifts at Coca-Cola’s plant in Dongen, so a third pallet no longer has to be scanned by hand, saving up to six minutes a lorry. Tool tracking at ASCO, who machine parts for Airbus and Boeing and could not tell two similar items apart in their own database. Proof of delivery for Ireland’s largest medical testing laboratory, replacing paper.

Where I came into it

This was my first internship. Fontys accredits ninety days of professional experience, and the brief was to put what I had been taught against real business problems in my own domain, which was information and communication technology.

The marketing department initiated the project and my company mentor ran it single-handed. I reported to her and worked on the project alongside whatever else needed doing that week. The initial situation had no deliverables defined at all, which sounds like freedom and is mostly not: it means the first real task is working out what would count as having done the job.

I knew very little about Salesforce going in. That was read as an opportunity rather than a gap, and the learning came out of documentation, desk research and talking to certified people. The single most useful day was a Salesforce convention at the Amsterdam RAI in March, where I found one of the better third-party tools the business case later weighed.

The problem, which was two problems

The first is the one everybody notices. Zetes holds a large customer database and runs leads and opportunities in Salesforce, but information from anything that is not already electronic, a business card, a trade show, a conference, has to be put in by hand. The existing route was to type it into a spreadsheet and bulk-upload that with Dataloader.io.

The second is the one that does the damage. The data was not uniform. Inconsistencies, duplicates, and no agreed criteria for anybody entering a record to follow. Users were also responsible for keeping it valid, which in practice meant looking people up on LinkedIn to check whether their role had changed. Automating the first problem without fixing the second would have meant filling the system faster with data of the same quality.

The question

Everything resolved to one question, and the report keeps coming back to it: how to improve the Salesforce experience at Zetes. The stated ideal was a self-replenishing Salesforce, where sales and marketing spend close to no time maintaining customer information because maintaining it is not what they are for. The realistic goal was to make the experience more cost and time effective without making it more complicated, and to analyse the scenario in which that is possible.

Choosing a tool, and being able to show why

Eighteen business applications on the Salesforce AppExchange, all of them in the sales intelligence category, which is where data enrichment lives. Picking one by preference would have been quicker and would not have survived the first question from anyone holding a budget. So the choice ran through a weighted scoring sheet instead.

A criterion scores its full weight when every one of its sub-tests passes, half when some of them do, and nothing when none do. Eighty per cent qualifies an application for investment. The interesting part is what the weights say: the top four, sixty per cent between them, are all about whether the thing works and whether anybody can stand to use it. Price is ten.

Fixing the data, not just the entry

The second problem got its own deliverable: a best practice manual. It covers accounts and contacts, reports and report types, dynamic dashboards and the Salesforce interfaces, so somebody new has somewhere to read rather than starting the same desk research over again. It shows how to use Dataloader.io to get a CSV into Salesforce as accounts and contacts. And it carries a step-by-step routine for entering leads from a trade show, written for the salespeople who actually come back with the cards.

The part that mattered most to data quality was the dullest to write: conventions for names and telephone numbers, so that two people entering the same company produce the same record. Plus the small recoveries, such as reconstructing a missing email address from the pattern of the other addresses in the same account.

NCRM

The idea, the interface and the concept were mine, put to my mentor as a rough design early on and taken further once it was well received. Neat CRM, because neat is what it was supposed to be: a form for each category, a side navigation, icons you do not have to decode, and only the fields without which a contact or an account would be incomplete in Salesforce. Every user signs in with their Salesforce credentials; the administrator adds and removes people.

Behind that sit more than seventy functional requirements, specified so they interlock without contradicting each other and without leaving a gap for the user to fall into. It integrates with Salesforce object to object, which is why the fields in its forms carry identical names. My university tutor’s line about this, which I have not stopped quoting, was that a simple interface means complex functionality underneath. Building the specification felt less like writing a document than like designing a board game.

What shipped

NCRM was a design. It stayed a design: I could not find a way to build it on Force.com, and integrating directly with the public Zetes website had already been tried and abandoned because it let anyone with no business potential register themselves into Salesforce.

So the workaround became a deliverable in its own right. A Google Form for accounts and another for contacts, both reachable from a small site built on the Oracle Eloqua access my mentor granted. The responses land in a Google Sheet shaped exactly like the Dataloader.io template, so the upload needs no rework. A Google Apps script called removeDuplicates strips repeats on the fields that identify a record: first and last name for contacts, account name for accounts.

It is not the system. It is the thing that reduced the administrative pile after every trade show while the system was still a specification, and it doubled as the demonstration of what the specification was for.

How it landed

Nine out of ten. The stakeholders, the university among them, were satisfied with the research products and the recommendations. My mentor’s own recommendation was to write one more thing, a user manual for NCRM that would double as a development aid, which fell outside the scope in the project initiation document and was left deliberately flexible.

What I took from it

That estimating a deadline is harder than it looks. I left myself room on every deliverable and still found some of them needed more than I had given them, usually the ones that turned out to need hands on the system before anything sensible could be said about it.

That feedback on something tangible beats discussion about nothing. Every one of the five deliverables went through repeated rounds with my mentor, and the rounds were only useful once there was an object to react to.

And that a design and a working thing are different outcomes, the second one harder and worth more. The next placement I took, I went in determined to leave something running behind me.
