Counting before choosing: object-based estimation for a data-warehouse migration

How to price two ways of moving a warehouse by estimating every object in it, and how to say afterwards where the difference between the routes comes from. A case study on a fictitious organisation, with the method set out in full.

  • SubjectEffort estimation
  • AudienceDelivery leads and budget holders
  • DatedNovember 2026
  • LengthTen chapters, an appendix and a workbook

The decision

Northmere Components Group has asked each of its subsidiaries to converge on one group data platform, HORIZON, which loads and transforms data in a single step on Azure Databricks, with dbt for the models and Azure Data Factory for orchestration. Northmere West runs its own warehouse, ATLAS, a two-step Data Vault 2.0 design on SQL Server and SSIS. The question put to its delivery team was not whether to move ATLAS, but which of two routes to take and what each would cost.

Lift and shift moves ATLAS onto cloud infrastructure with SQL Server, SSIS and the two-step design intact, so the difference between the routes prices the change of pattern and nothing else. Translation rebuilds every object in the single-step pattern that HORIZON expects. The organisation and every figure in the case are fictitious; the method is the one the paper sets out to generalise.

Counting objects rather than guessing totals

The paper is the practical sequel to an earlier one on top-down and bottom-up estimation, and it takes the bottom-up side. Instead of one figure for the whole migration, the warehouse is inventoried object by object, and each of its 286 storage objects is estimated on its own: 68 staging tables, 33 hubs, 30 links, 68 satellites, 24 reference tables and 63 marts or views.

Every object passes through the same ten tasks, from source analysis to deployment and hypercare, and each task is scored from 0 to 5 against a written rubric. A score of 0 means the task is fully reused or automated, and a score of 5 means heavy logic, dependencies across objects or unclear requirements. The scores are driven by what can be counted: attributes, sources and joins, business rules, the kind of history kept, volume, downstream dependants and known data-quality issues. The same object is scored once for each route, because a satellite that is simple to rehost can be complex to redraw.

Work that belongs to no object, such as environments, security, deployment pipelines and programme management, is kept out of the object model and estimated separately from the top down. Folding it into the objects would hide it inside numbers that nobody could then challenge.

From a score to hours

A lookup matrix turns a score into hours. Its rows are the ten tasks and its columns are the six levels. Each cell is the task's base hours at level 3, scaled by a level curve that grows faster than the level, so that a level 5 task costs 12 times a level 1 task. The translation route reads a second matrix, in which each task carries a stated relative-effort factor for the target platform.

# one object on one route: ten lookups, one for each task
hours = sum(H[route][t][score[obj][t][route]] for t in range(10))

# both matrices share the base hours B and the level curve lam
H['legacy'][t][c] = round(B[t] * lam[c] * k, 2)            # k = 1.295
H['target'][t][c] = round(B[t] * lam[c] * k * f[t], 2)     # f = relative effort

# hours to man-days, and man-days to months for a team of n
man_days = hours / 8
months   = man_days / (20 * n * (1 - 0.15 * (n - 1)))

The base hours are a reconstruction of judgement, so the matrix was tested against what the original build of ATLAS actually cost. A sample of 45 objects, stratified by type, had timesheet hours on record. Scored as they had been built, the uncalibrated matrix predicted 831.8 hours against 1,076.9 actual, an estimate 22.8 per cent short. A shortfall of that kind is what Kahneman and Tversky called the planning fallacy, and it is the reason the matrix is calibrated before it is used.

The calibration factor is the ratio of actual to predicted hours across the sample, k = 1.295, with a 90 per cent bootstrap interval from 1.25 to 1.338. Applied to the sample, it brings the mean absolute percentage error down from 20.3 to 11.8 per cent. One factor is deliberately plain: the ratio by object type runs from 1.059 for hubs to 1.353 for reference tables, and the paper reports it as a diagnostic rather than fitting six factors to 45 objects.

Where the difference sits

Summed over all 286 objects, lift and shift comes to 521.6 man-days and translation to 675.6, a difference of 154.0 man-days and a premium of 29.5 per cent. The chart takes that difference apart by task.

Tasks 2, 4 and 5 add 159.3 man-days between them, which is 103.5 per cent of the whole difference, while the other seven tasks give back 5.3. The premium is therefore the price of the change of pattern and very little else, which is the fact a choice between the routes most needs.

The premium is not even across the estate. Object by object its median is 25.5 per cent, with half of all objects between 3.8 and 54.4 per cent, and by subject area it runs from 12.3 per cent for reference data to 44.0 per cent for product. That spread suggests a third route. A subject area is translated when its premium is at most 30 per cent and lifted otherwise, and each lifted area pays a bridge of 6 man-days for the interface that exposes its marts to the translated estate.

The hybrid comes to 592.5 man-days and puts 49.0 per cent of the objects on the target pattern at once. The refactoring of the lifted areas, 107.1 man-days, is reported separately as a later decision rather than hidden in the total. The case recommends the hybrid because it converges where convergence is cheap and defers it where it is not.

One engineer would need 29.6 months for the hybrid route. Two would need 17.4 and three 14.1, not a half and a third of the first figure, because each added engineer costs 15 per cent of capacity in coordination. That is the effect Brooks described, and the model states it as a parameter rather than leaving it to optimism.

How firm the answer is

An estimate built from scores is only as firm as the scores. The chart varies five inputs one at a time around the hybrid route's 17.4 months for two engineers.

Moving every non-zero score one level lower or higher swings the duration from 11.0 to 30.0 months, while the calibration factor at either end of its interval moves it only from 16.8 to 18.0. Review time therefore belongs on the scores, and the method makes that possible: each score sits on a named object and a named task, at a rubric level that a second person can check.

The paper closes on the governance of the estimate. Each score records who set it, when and why; scores are revised as discovery proceeds; and actual effort is tracked against the estimate object by object while the work is done. Those habits are what turn a contested architectural choice into a decision that both sides can inspect line by line.
