Corporate Restructuring in the Digital Age

What makes a company restructure, worked through one case: a Danish hearing aid maker that was analogue in a digitising market, growing two per cent a year while its market grew six.

  • CaseOticon A/S, Denmark

What restructuring actually is

Reorganising the legal, ownership or operational structure of a company to make it profitable, or simply better arranged for what it now has to do. Put that way it sounds procedural, and in an informal setting it is: moving the furniture to get more out of a room is the same act.

It stops being procedural inside a legally bound institution, because the thing being rearranged is people. People of different competences, nationalities and educational backgrounds, who may simply not cooperate. Small problems chain into larger ones until the structure itself is what is at risk, and people are generally less prepared to handle chaos than they think.

Noise

As the problems pile up they make noise, and the noise carries through the whole organisation. Left alone long enough, either a change or a compromise becomes inevitable. What tips leadership towards change rather than compromise is usually two things: reputation, which is a strong incentive at that level, and the financial consequence of having done nothing.

Slice the elephant

There is rarely one big problem. There is a multitude of small problems of similar nature that together look like one. Cut it up, solve the pieces, and integrate the solutions as you go; what comes out is one answer to the apparent single problem.

A restructuring initiative is a call for help from an organisation that is unstructured enough that it cannot compete, while still performing well enough to avoid bankruptcy. There is no one-size-fits-all answer to that, and the moment at which the problem was noticed is itself a variable, so the approach has to be calibrated to the scale of what is wrong. Calibration needs experience and data, which is where an outside consultant earns the fee.

Why the digital age changes the question

Moore’s law says the transistor count on a dense integrated circuit doubles about every two years. The paper does not leave that as an abstraction; it lists what the resulting computing power had already produced by the time it was written. Four point one three billion internet users in 2019. More than fifty billion IP-enabled sensors by 2020, with RFID tags down to five cents each and projections running from one to ten trillion sensors by 2025. Ninety per cent of the world’s stock of data generated in the preceding decade, ninety-nine per cent of it digitised and over half of it IP-enabled, meaning it can be put on the internet.

A company that has sat comfortably in one section of a market for years finds the leap hardest, because the difficulty is in scaling its own structure to a bigger customer base. The technical and communication problems surface one after another, and daily operations stop without systems in place. At that point the company either spends its own time and money on restructuring or hires someone.

Oticon

A Danish hearing aid company, very traditional, strictly departmentalised, hierarchical lines of management. Comfortable in the state-subsidised markets of Scandinavia and Northern Europe and weak in North America and the East. Siemens, Sony and Philips were digitising hearing aids sooner than it was.

It tried the obvious thing first. Cutting expenses and laying people off produced an overall efficiency gain of two per cent, in a market growing at six. The deeper problem is that its own structure was what prevented it from seeing the rest: that it lacked resources, that it lacked technological excellence, and that it needed to be in other markets sooner than it thought.

What Kolind did

Under a newly appointed president, Lars Kolind, the change mechanism was stated in three lines: lay out a plan to reach the desired state with strategies at several levels, anticipate resistance before it arrives and prepare for it, then overcome it and put the plan back in motion. The interesting part is what sat under those three lines.

The long-term strategy was to redesign the business to be more creative, faster and more cost-effective than the big players, precisely to compensate for the constraints the structure had imposed. With it came a new idea of what the company was: knowledge-based rather than product-based, which the paper puts as a shift from an engine to a brain. It gave up technological isolation and invested in hearing clinics, to understand how customers actually live, and assembled a mix of expertise across micro-mechanics, microchip design, audiology, psychology, marketing, manufacturing and logistics.

The short-term strategy

This is the part people remember. Departments and job titles were removed outright, and all activity became projects started and pursued by groupings of interested people. Jobs were redesigned into fluid combinations of functions fitted to each person’s needs and capabilities. Every vestige of the formal office was eradicated and replaced by open space full of workstations anyone could use. Informal, face-to-face dialogue replaced the memo as the accepted channel.

Structurally it is a combination of the planned and the emergent approach, because what was being changed was structure, culture, learning and managerial behaviour all at once. It also carries Theory E and Theory O together: hard systems and soft culture addressed simultaneously, incentives used to reinforce the change rather than to drive it, and spontaneity planned for rather than hoped for.

Resistance, anticipated rather than discovered

The resistance arrived as expected. Employees found the loss of routine, of clear authority relationships and of certainty hard to cope with. Managers lost their power base, their monopoly on information and their status symbols, which is three separate losses and they are rarely counted separately.

What the paper is sharpest about is the two mistakes managers make here. The first is picking one method of handling resistance and using it regardless of the situation. The second is approaching change in a disjointed, incremental way that is not part of a considered strategy. Oticon avoided both, and held an ultimatum in reserve for the case where nothing else worked.

In practice: small groups assigned only to the transformational work, designing the new electronic infrastructure, selecting an architect, finding a site for the new head office. All staff given IT training. And then, if none of it landed, change or leave.

The question the paper is actually asking

Not how Oticon restructured, which is the case study, but why it had to restructure in order to get into the digital age at all. The answer the paper arrives at is a single inversion: you cannot restructure the digital age to fit your company. Only the other way round.

Kolind’s own words carry it better than mine. He had looked at technology, at audiology, at distribution strength, at everything, and found nothing the company could do better than the competition. What he proposed instead was to design a new way of running a business, significantly more creative, faster and more cost-effective than the big players, and to let that compensate for the lack of technological excellence, of capital and of resources.

There is one more line of his that explains why the open-plan, no-titles structure did not simply collapse. The company was built on the assumption that it employs adults, and everything it does rests on that assumption: staff are not treated as children but as responsible adults.

What I recommended

Sustaining a success is harder than producing one, because the temptation is to reinvent rather than to continue. Oticon had already identified its own root cause, which was a sense of permanency in how the company did its daily business, and answered it by randomising workstation allocation between floors until that became part of the culture.

The recommendation, written from a consultant’s chair rather than the company’s, is not to deviate from what worked, but to prepare for the next wave the way they were not prepared for the last one: invest in the communication systems and internal relationships that the structure depends on, use a sense-making framework to classify a change before prescribing a response to it, build the programmes that develop collective intelligence and group dynamics, and clear resistance deliberately rather than by ultimatum a second time.
