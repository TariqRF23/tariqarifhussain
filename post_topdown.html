Top-down or Bottom-up: which approach is better?

A hypothesis that the more users a feature-rich application takes on, the harder it pulls towards one approach or the other. The paper tests it across management, system design, computer science and service architecture, and ends up somewhere less satisfying and more useful than either end.

  • SubjectSoftware architecture and management
  • FocusMicroservices
  • MethodInductive, on limited literature
  • Written2022

The two approaches, and the one in between

Both are general strategies for processing information and knowledge, which is why they turn up in software, in systems theory and in management without anyone translating between the fields. Top-down formulates an overview of the system without emphasising each detail. Bottom-up starts from the individual parts and links them into a whole.

In management the same principle gets different names. Top-down is the directive that originates with leadership, which is why it is called the traditional approach: a manager decides and the decision trickles down a hierarchy. Bottom-up is more democratic and collaborative, involving the team or the organisation as a whole, and it is common wherever disruption and innovation are the point.

Where each one earns its place

In system design the distinction is practical. Top-down needs extensive planning and research before a product exists, which suits a large and complex system where the whole has to be understood first. Bottom-up suits experimental development, where the parts are what you are learning about, and a tight budget, where you cannot afford the planning phase before anything works. Collaborative effort pulls differently again, because parts can be built in parallel by people who do not need the whole picture.

Microservices, which is the real subject

Microservices are finely grained services, a distant cousin of service-oriented architecture, each designed to work independently on a single task. The property that matters is loose coupling: two systems are loosely coupled when a change to the design, implementation or behaviour of one does not affect the other.

Which brings the question every organisation that wants them has to answer. How do you divide a monolith into manageable pieces? There are two answers, and each is attractive for a reason that has nothing to do with the other.

Top-down decomposes functionally, starting at the business level, separating by domain or by business capability. Its appeal is that a non-technical decision maker can read it. Its failures are concrete: a microservice per function becomes unmanageable at any scale, each service has to arrange its own infrastructure so the cost climbs, and the resulting service may not match how the development and support teams are actually organised, so shipping one of them needs several teams to coordinate.

Bottom-up is purely technical, defining services from the development resources that exist: bundle the deposit functions together, or split by stack and make a service per technology. Its appeal is efficient use of the people you have. Its failures are the mirror image: a single service can span several lines of business, so ownership blurs, and release timing stops lining up, so an aggressive business unit waits on a development team serving someone else.

The hybrid, concretely

Not a compromise but an order of operations. Begin with a domain model or a functional decomposition, which engages every stakeholder the service is meant to benefit. Then optimise the resulting inventory technically, separating or folding functions together from an operational point of view, to decide how they will actually be managed.

The advantage is that both IT and the business are involved at the highest level and from the beginning, so the services make sense commercially and are still efficient to run day to day.

What it concludes

The hypothesis was that scale forces a choice. It does not. The hybrid is the clear winner, and the debate between the two camps is useful mainly because each side’s criticism of the other is accurate: top-down is right that bottom-up carries excessive updates and rework, and bottom-up is right that top-down carries extensive planning and strict policy enforcement. The decision sits in a grey area where contextual demand overrides technical preference.

Where it goes next

The paper ends by naming the larger question it kept running into, which is digitalisation itself, and the role of large technology companies in it. Strip away the social media and the search engines and what is underneath is cloud computing, and that is where the research was headed next: underrated technologies in that space such as OpenStack against the conventional AWS and Azure, how to circumvent vendor lock-in, and the markets that have not yet had the spotlight.

That turned into the multi-cloud work that follows this paper, which is the same question asked of one company with a budget attached.
