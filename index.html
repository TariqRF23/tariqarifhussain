Backwards, and slowly. The system is carrying the business while you change it, so the first job is not the target design, it is an honest inventory of what the current one actually does. On the warehouse move I am planning now, every object in it was catalogued and scored for complexity before anyone drew the new architecture. These projects usually fail in the same place: the new design quietly omits something the old one did at two in the morning on the last working day of the month.

Then simplify, but only where the simplification can be proved. That warehouse loses an entire layer, two steps become one, and the rule governing it is that every piece of business logic survives the translation. A target architecture is not better for having fewer boxes on the slide. It is better when it does the same work with less to maintain and you can point at where each rule went.

Then sequence it so that each phase stands on its own and nothing is switched off until the thing replacing it has been proved in use. The human risk is a risk like any other and usually the larger one: on that project it was a scope ambiguity between two teams in different countries, settled in workshops while it was still a question rather than later as a change request. The same discipline applies at a much smaller scale, where a version upgrade or a configuration change has to land with no customer-facing downtime.


DeskOps is a helpdesk that grew into an ERP, written from scratch, solo, since September 2024. It carries the full incident lifecycle, role-based access across support tiers, SLA enforcement on business-hour calendars, double-entry financials, billing, timesheets, knowledge management and KPI analytics. Measured from its own repository, that is 16 core modules, 78 screens, 590 API endpoints, 306 database objects and more than 1,400 commits. The whole stack, database and inference engine included, sits in one folder and runs with the network unplugged.

I build it because operations work shows you exactly where service management leaks, and an opinion about that is worth more once you have had to implement it. Separation of duties, a change that cannot skip its approval, an SLA clock that stops at closing time: each of those exists in DeskOps as code I had to make work, not as a slide. There is a full write-up of the system, screen by screen, at DeskOps.


I am a member of BMW NL's IT AI Core Team, which evaluates where AI genuinely improves a process and where it only appears to. My own prototype flags tickets heading for an SLA breach, surfaces knowledge-base articles that match the context, and proposes escalation paths. It is a proof of concept, and I describe it as one.

The line is that nothing is trusted to the model behaving well. In DeskOps four disciplines are enforced around it. Answers are grounded in indexed evidence or not made at all. The everyday work runs on a model on the same machine as the data, with cloud models as a contingency rather than a default. Anything cloud-bound passes a redaction gate that strips names, emails and identifiers and hashes each removal, so an audit trail exists without a copy of the secret. Claims carry citations that a screening pass can strike out, and exact work such as arithmetic and dates goes to deterministic tools rather than the model's mental maths.

Guardrails below the model, schema allow-lists, read-only execution accounts and scoped, revocable tokens are what enforce those disciplines. The Areas of Interest list above says where I rate myself on each of them.

By starting with the number the business already feels, not with the technology I would like to use. The warehouse migration was never argued as "Snowflake is better". It was argued object by object: every one of them counted and scored for complexity, an effort figure that could be traced back to those scores, a phased plan and a critical path that anyone in the room was free to take apart. An estimate built that way is far harder to wave away than a conviction, because it answers the only question that matters: where did that number come from?

Then what it buys and when it arrives: a layer of the warehouse removed, every piece of business logic preserved through the translation, and a date against each phase. Treating a claim as something to be checked rather than asserted. It came out of a STEM education, where a fact is established by testing rather than by agreement, and it is the one habit I apply identically to a production incident, an estimate and an answer from a model.

In practice it is unglamorous. A root-cause review that has to survive the next occurrence of the same incident. An estimate built object by object, so that anyone can ask where a number came from. An AI feature made to cite its sources, because an answer that cannot be traced is an opinion with good grammar. The industry calls the state you get into doing this deep work. What it actually is, is refusing to move on while something is still unexplained.

Everything else I would list follows from it: critical and analytical thinking, reasoning, and being able to explain any of it to somebody who does not share the background. That last one decides whether the work travels beyond the person who did it, which is usually what separates a good piece of work from a useful one.
