How Oticon successfully implemented strategic changes for global market leadership in hearing aid technology

The same company as the restructuring paper, taken through a different course and a different question: not why a company restructures, but how the resistance to it is handled, and what keeps the result alive afterwards.

  • CourseInternational Change Management and Outplacement
  • ProgrammeMSc Corporate Management
  • UniversityUniversity of Europe for Applied Sciences
  • FormatPresentation, seventeen slides

Where the company stood

Oticon made hearing aids in Denmark, against Siemens, Sony and Philips. It was analogue at the moment the trend turned digital. It was strong in the state-subsidised markets of Scandinavia and Northern Europe and weak in America and the Far East. It was traditional, departmentalised and slow-moving, with no sustainable advantage over anyone larger, and a growth rate that was not survivable.

The conventional answer had already been tried: price reduction, layoffs and efficiency, run from the top. That produced two per cent sales growth a year in a market growing at six. The arithmetic is the whole argument for doing something else.

Two constraints made it worse. The need to get into other markets was only recognised late, after Lars Kolind arrived as president. And underneath that sat a plain lack of technological excellence, of capital, and of resources generally.

The mechanism

Three steps, and the middle one is the one most change programmes skip. Lay out the plan for the desired state, with strategies formulated at several levels. Anticipate the resistance before it appears and prepare for it. Then overcome it and set the plan back in motion.

Long term: deciding what kind of company to be

Porter’s generic strategies, narrowed by elimination. Cost leadership was not available against companies that size. Focus would have meant accepting the corner it was already stuck in. What was left was product differentiation, and it is visible in the mission statement that came out of it: to help people with hearing difficulties to live as they wish, with the hearing they have.

Concretely that meant redesigning the business to be more creative, faster and more cost-effective than the big players, adopting a vision of a knowledge-based organisation that behaves more like a brain than a machine, abandoning technological isolation in favour of a system built with hearing clinics to understand how customers live, and staffing it with a mix of micro-mechanics, microchip design, audiology, psychology, marketing, manufacturing, logistics and all-round service.

Short term: the structure itself

A hybrid of planned and emergent organisational change, aimed at structure, culture, learning and managerial behaviour together, heavily influenced by the combined Theory E and Theory O model, and contingent by design: whatever method was best available.

Departments and job titles disappeared and all activity became projects, initiated and pursued by groupings of interested people. Jobs were redesigned into fluid, unique combinations of functions suited to each employee. Every vestige of the formal office was eradicated and replaced by open space filled with workstations anyone could use. Informal, face-to-face dialogue replaced the memo.

Preparing for the resistance

This is what the presentation does that the paper does not. The preparation starts with a stakeholder analysis, then calibrates the approach to particular stakeholders in particular positions to get their buy-in, then pinpoints the cause of resistance in each case and applies the method that fits that cause. Five methods, and one thing held back.

What the resistance actually was

Hard to cope with the loss of routine, of clear authority relationships, and of certainty. And for managers specifically: the loss of a power base, of an information monopoly, and of status symbols.

The preparation against it was concrete. Small groups assigned only to transformational work, designing the new electronic infrastructure, selecting an architect, locating a site for the new head office. IT skills training for all staff. And the ultimatum: accept the new arrangements or leave.

The result

Why it worked, as the critical analysis sets it out: a solid strategic setup and vision, effective handling of resistance, strong transformational leadership, the ultimatum, and architectural changes to the workplace that made the rest possible. Danish culture helped, with its equality and lack of formality as fertile ground. And human values were enforced rather than stated: freedom, responsibility, fair reward for contribution, partnership, improvement, independence.

The value came from somewhere specific. Changing the work environment changed how flexible people were willing to be, and that showed up as tasks being taken on and finished faster.

Keeping it alive

People were moved between floors so that no sense of permanency could settle back in. The line that captures the philosophy is blunt: to keep the company alive, one of the jobs of top management is to keep it dis-organised. Under a later president, Niels Jacobsen, the same philosophy widened out into society, ethics and the environment.

Where it stands now

The overall management ideology and practices have largely stayed as they were. Some roles have been professionalised, project manager in particular, so fewer people are assigned as team leaders. The roles of functional experts have been preserved inside the more traditional business units and staff functions. And the organisation is still working towards being paperless.

Innovate or extinct

The forward plan, which is the part that is mine rather than the case’s. Given how well the approach worked, I would not deviate from sustaining it. What I would add sits in four places.

Invest in effective communication systems and strong internal relationships, because that is what a structure without departments actually runs on. Use the Cynefin model to recognise what kind of change is in front of you before deciding how to handle it. Design programmes that improve collective intelligence, group dynamics and team development. And clear resistance to change actively, through Kotter’s eight steps, rather than relying on an ultimatum a second time.

Once that communication-based internal structure is solid, the same attention goes outward: to relationships with suppliers, and to partnerships with public organisations.
