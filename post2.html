Graduation internship · 2018

Accelerated use of Microsoft Dynamics NAV within the Antenna Company B.V.

The company wanted its ERP data in a dashboard. The database turned out to be unreachable: it is hosted by a third party whose policy refuses customers SQL access, and there is no route to the figures except the ERP itself. So the project stopped trying to change the system and changed how people used it.

  • ClientThe Antenna Company Nederland B.V.
  • WhereHigh Tech Campus, Eindhoven
  • When5 February to 5 July 2018
  • ForFontys University of Applied Sciences
  • RoleIntern, five stakeholders, ninety working days

The company

The Antenna Company was formed in 2013 to commercialise a geometry-based antenna technology, built on patented Super-Formula design principles and covering everything from sub-gigahertz to millimetre wave. It sells to the manufacturers who put antennas into Wi-Fi, GPS and cellular products. The design centre is in Eindhoven, with sales offices in the United States and Asia.

It trades as three fiscal entities, and that matters here more than it looks. A customer order arrives at the international entity; the Dutch entity places the manufacturing order with contract manufacturers in Asia. The two entities book purchase and sales orders against each other inside NAV, which is why so many of the figures the business is run on only exist as a join between two reports.

The assignment

Accelerate use of Microsoft Dynamics NAV 2016 among the people who depend on it in sales, operations, supply chain and finance. At the top level that meant getting stakeholders to engage with their own ERP data. Underneath it meant a specific list of key performance indicators, gathered from them one at a time.

Some of those stakeholders used NAV every day. Some barely used it at all. They knew what they wanted; what did not exist was a way of asking them for it, so the first job was to build one. Requirements came out of one-to-one sessions, group discussions and Skype calls with the people who work from other offices.

The wall

I came in from a background in data visualisation and assumed Power BI. That assumption lasted until the authentication dialog refused the connection.

NAV is supplied to the company by a Microsoft partner, and the database behind it is hosted in the cloud by a third company. That host does not permit SQL authentication to customers, for their own risk management: direct manipulation of the database is how systems break. There is no way to the data except through NAV. Two routes out of it were offered and neither was mine to take: migrate the database onto the company’s own servers, which exposes it to loss during installation, or subscribe to Power BI Pro and pay for the access at a service tier.

Finding this late in the cycle is the uncomfortable part. The scope had been written on the assumption that the figures were reachable, and most of the research had gone into customising system reports against what users expected, in pursuit of a loophole that turned out not to exist. Naming the constraint, rather than continuing to look for a way round it, is what let the project deliver anything at all.

The change of strategy

If the system cannot be changed, change the way it is used. That is the whole pivot, and everything after it follows from the sentence.

It also changed the shape of the work: out of a linear plan that assumed each stage would succeed, and into a cycle of small complete results. Twelve of those results are recorded in the report, each one finished before the next began.

Twenty-six KPIs, and where each one lives

The requested indicators were delivery performance and order confirmation intervals, book to bill, gross margin by product, segment and region, open and unfulfilled orders, margin by product, segment and region, inventory valuation, the cash conversion cycle, and forecast coverage with a revenue outlook. Definitions came from the stakeholders themselves and, where they differed, from Bernard Marr’s book on key performance indicators, borrowed from my company coach.

Then the real work: taking the reports those people already ran, removing the duplicates, and finding which fields in which report actually assemble each indicator. The result is a map, and the map is the deliverable that made the rest possible. The bowtie means a join: the figure does not exist in either report on its own.

G4-NAV

Gear Fourth, Navision. A standard operating procedure rather than a system: step-by-step walkthroughs that build each KPI report from scratch inside NAV, each step carrying its own screenshot and its own estimated completion time. Layouts for Mac and for Windows in one document, because not everybody in the company is on Windows. Excel modules embedded in it so the arithmetic happens where the user already is. NAV’s own reports sorted and modified to match the procedure. Approved, with user feedback written back into it.

It is a less impressive object than a dashboard and a more useful one, because it works for someone who has never opened NAV and it does not need anybody’s permission to run.

What was in scope, and what was not

The scope was reviewed with everyone involved, under MoSCoW, once the constraint was known. Putting the Power BI setup explicitly in the last of the four was the point of the exercise: it stopped being an unmet expectation and became a decision.

Must
G4-NAV carrying the procedure for every KPI report: open orders, back orders, book to bill, shipments, delivery performance, order confirmation interval, inventory turns, inventory by value, gross margin % by product and customer, purchase spend, margin and sales by product, segment and region.
Should
Those reports embedded in G4-NAV rather than described beside it.
Could
Power BI visualisation, a forecasting module on the revenue outlook, every report on the stakeholder’s own schedule with a dashboard inside NAV, and the cash conversion cycle as a year by month.
Will not
A Power BI Pro setup for users to visualise, style and schedule their own reports. The access it needs is the access that was refused.

Two things found on the way

NAV can schedule a report to go out on a recurrence. Asked to set that up for the open orders report, I found it did not reliably arrive: the report simply failed to appear in the Report Inbox, more than once. Worth knowing before anybody depends on it.

Several stakeholders work on Macs, which cannot run the Windows desktop client, and switching between NAV companies through the web client threw errors. The way round it was to construct the URL directly, which is in G4-NAV as a documented procedure rather than as a trick one person knew.

One hypothesis, and how it was tested

Margin should be the difference between what a product sold for and what it cost. In NAV those two figures sit in different reports, Customer/Item Sales and Vendor/Item Purchases, so the question was whether subtracting one from the other is actually the margin or merely looks like it.

I tested it against real March 2018 figures. For a month in which one product sold, the quantity matched across both reports, the vendor cost matched the opening inventory, and the difference between sales and vendor cost equalled the difference between gross profit margin and closing inventory value. Cost of goods sold came out the same by both routes, against the standard definition of opening inventory plus purchases less closing inventory. The hypothesis held, and the cost figure in Vendor/Item Purchases and the opening inventory value in Inventory Valuation are the same measure. That is the kind of thing worth checking before thirteen other figures are built on top of it.

What I recommended

In the short term, supervised SQL access, or work on copies of the database: join the reports by query along the lines of the map, assemble the KPIs, and then put them into the production environment with the layouts users asked for, including the year-by-month view they kept requesting.

In the longer term, buy the access. Power BI Pro brings scheduled refresh, automatic delivery and real visualisation. But access to the data is access to the data, and that is a conversation about who in the company is allowed to see what, which the key users need to have with each other before anybody signs an invoice.

How it landed

The work was passed. The procedure went to the people it was written for, with training and a survey to close it out on their opinion rather than mine. Five rehearsal runs went into the all-stakeholder presentation in April, each one assessed by the primary stakeholders; the update in May was given to four of them with the CEO on a conference call, and the rule for those was one or two slides, no more.

What it changed

This was the most intense stretch of learning I had had, and the point at which I turned hard towards the work I do now. It left a sense of having far more to do than the problems in front of me. That feeling lasted the whole of my master’s and has driven my formal education ever since.
