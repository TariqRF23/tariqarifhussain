How blockchain based smart-contracts can improve invoicing accuracy

Roughly a tenth of freight invoices carry inaccurate data. This thesis asks whether blockchain based smart contracts fix that, and answers it narrowly on purpose: not as a cure, but in the specific places where the evidence supports it.

  • ProgrammeMSc Corporate Management
  • UniversityUniversity of Europe for Applied Sciences, Germany
  • Submitted2020
  • Result95 out of 100

The question

How do blockchain based smart contracts improve invoicing accuracy? Underneath it sit two sub-questions, and the structure of the whole thesis follows from them. What is actually wrong with invoicing today, and why does the shipping and freight industry have it worst? And what are the advantages and disadvantages of applying blockchain to it, which cases are the relevant ones, and is it worth the risk the technology brings with it?

That last clause matters. A blockchain key that is lost is lost: the account it opens cannot be recovered. Any argument for adoption has to carry that, and the thesis says so in its first chapter rather than its last.

What a smart contract actually is

Code that facilitates, verifies or executes part of an agreement between parties once the terms are met, stored and run on a platform like Ethereum. The logic is the same conditional construction any programmer would recognise, written in languages such as Solidity: if the condition holds, then this; otherwise, that.

The thesis uses a crowdfunding example to make it concrete, and it is the clearest short definition I know. A team sets a minimum it needs to start. The contract holds every supporter’s money. If the minimum is reached, the funds go to the team; if it is not, they go back to the supporters. Nobody adjudicates. That is what self-enforcing means, and in a commercial setting it extends to issuing a penalty when a clause is breached.

Which kind of chain matters as much as the contract on it, and the difference comes down entirely to who is allowed to validate a block.

The consortium case is the one the trade argument needs. A handful of equally powerful parties validating, which is a reasonable description of a shipper, a carrier, a forwarder and a bank who need a shared record but are not going to let any one of them keep it.

Why invoices go wrong

Moving a container is not one transaction. Terminal operators, trucking companies, banks, insurers, customs authorities, the carrier and the freight forwarder all exchange information about the same shipment, much of it on paper because regulation requires it. A single refrigerated load travelling from East Africa to Europe passes through close to thirty transitions and more than two hundred separate interactions.

Each party bills from its own records. Freight invoices carry accessorial charges on top of the move itself, calculated against things like miles driven and idle time, so the two sides are not merely copying a number from a contract. The industry estimate is that about a tenth of freight invoices contain inaccurate data.

The sharpest finding in the research is that this is not mainly a software problem. It got worse as back-office functions were outsourced or cut: the cheaper staff who replaced experienced people have no relationship with the container line or its clients, rate changes move faster than anybody can check them, and carriers under pressure optimise for issuing invoices rather than for issuing correct ones. Disputing an error is hard enough that some shippers stop bothering, and pay anyway within a tolerance they have set themselves.

Accenture put numbers on what that costs. Third-party audit and payment providers spend a quarter of their time managing exceptions; some organisations carry around two hundred thousand invoices outstanding because no record can be found of the service they are supposed to match. In oil and gas alone, Accenture expected at least five per cent of annual freight spend to be recoverable through better invoice accuracy and fewer overpayments.

The paradox

Boston Consulting Group surveyed more than a hundred companies across air freight, courier and parcel, logistics, rail and shipping, and named what they found a blockchain paradox: the technology addresses precisely the pain the industry reports, and adoption lags anyway. Invoicing and payments came out among the high-priority use cases.

Their reasoning for why is the same reasoning the rest of this thesis rests on. Every party keeps its own records and its own ledger, so invoicing is paper-heavy and often manually keyed, and a reconciliation step has to run before anybody is paid, to catch mistakes and possible fraud. Remove the second ledger and the reconciliation has nothing left to do.

The frame

The thesis does not treat this as an IT problem, which is what keeps it honest. It anchors the argument in liquidity management, the working capital requirement and the operating cycle, and in economic theory about trade and comparative advantage. An inaccurate invoice is not a clerical annoyance; it is cash sitting in the wrong place for longer than it should, inside a relationship where one party knows more than the other.

That asymmetry is the recurring theme. Rivalry over who knows what, the thesis argues, can take down companies that are otherwise sound.

Three things that stop supply chain finance

Suppliers need financing because invoices and bills are not reconciled as they happen. The delay is the reason banks are in the picture at all, and invoice-based financing is an estimated eighty to ninety per cent of that market. So the question of whether a shared ledger helps is not abstract: it is a question about three specific obstacles.

The middle one is the subtlest and the thesis is careful with it. When a buyer shares some of the value captured with the financing institution, the arrangement can be reclassified on the balance sheet, which touches loan covenants, leverage and access to further credit. Accounting rules do not bend for a technology. What a distributed ledger changes is the work around them: the audit trail is immutable, so the manual audit can go, and complex transactions and internal controls take less time.

The method

The practical contribution is a way of finding the problem before it surfaces as a dispute. A strategic early warning system runs in three phases: gather the information, diagnose what it shows, formulate a strategy. On its own that is too general to act on, so it is modified twice.

The proactive method of fraud detection supplies the first phase with something concrete: understand the business, work out what frauds could exist in it, catalogue the symptoms each would leave, use technology to gather data about those symptoms, analyse, investigate. The Cynefin framework supplies the second, by sorting what kind of problem is in front of you before anybody prescribes a fix for it.

The Cynefin half is worth seeing, because it is what stops the method prescribing the same fix everywhere. It sorts a situation by what the relationship between cause and effect actually is, and each answer implies a different move.

The reason for the modification is stated plainly in the thesis and is the reason the model is a guideline rather than a procedure: the causes of invoice inaccuracy in one company will not be the causes in another, because they come out of whatever systems that company already has.

What the evidence shows

Literature was selected against a stated criterion and covers scientific papers alongside whitepapers published by organisations working on the problem. The gap the thesis names is real: there is very little academic work joining all four parts of the subject, so much of the evidence is industry research, and it is treated as such.

The strongest case is a deployed one. DHL Global Forwarding and Hewlett Packard Enterprise built a minimum viable product that went live on 1 July 2019 for one-off orders, the shipments their network can support but their standard contract does not cover. Invoices are issued automatically when proof of delivery is written to the chain, against the contractual payment terms. They report full invoice accuracy and the removal of the post-delivery checks that used to precede approval.

The thesis also records what it cannot verify: the press release gives no technical detail, so the process behind the claim cannot be checked. Saying that out loud, in the chapter that most supports the hypothesis, is the part I would still defend.

What it costs to run

The most useful piece of evidence in the whole literature review is an experimental model published through the IEEE, because it is the only one that lets you put a number on the argument. It digitises invoicing with a VAT component across four parties, the tax administrator, the seller, the buyer and the bank, on a private Ethereum chain. Invoices themselves are encrypted and held in a decentralised storage network over IPFS rather than on the chain, which is how it keeps the storage cost down.

That is the comparison the thesis makes, and it is careful about what it proves: a cost advantage in an experiment, scaling in direct proportion to the number of operations, on a chain whose running cost rose more than fortyfold in eighteen months. The cost case is real and it is not stable.

Where it is viable

Three places, each for the same reason: a figure that is currently calculated twice becomes a figure that exists once.

Freight bill audit and payment, where contracted rates and every movement of the freight sit on one shared history, and accessorials are computed from inputs both sides can see. Order-to-cash, where a smart contract settles or reserves funds against agreed terms and can post the entry in the ERP as an asset or a liability; a change to an order before shipment updates the shared record, adjusts the invoice and informs everyone at once, and an incomplete delivery adjusts the invoice by itself. And procure-to-pay, the same mechanism from the other side of the transaction.

A multi-criteria decision matrix and two decision trees close the argument, which is the part aimed at somebody who has to justify the spend rather than admire the technology.

What a company actually weighs

The decision matrix is the part written for whoever has to approve the spend. Six criteria, each broken into sub-criteria: cost as purchase, maintenance and extras; speed as encryption, transaction and query latency; privacy as security, anonymity and address tracking; logistics fit as whether it works with the internet of things, with other supply chains and with the process already in place; then functionality and whether developers can be hired at all.

Three decision makers compared them a pair at a time, with their preferences expressed as ranges rather than fixed scores, which is the point of running it as a fuzzy hierarchy: it takes some of the subjectivity out without pretending to remove it.

Cost at 0.42, which is more than the four criteria below it put together. Whatever the technology promises, the company in that study was asking first whether it fits the budget. Developer availability came last at 0.02, which is either confidence or an oversight, and the thesis does not pretend to know which.

Why it stalls anyway

Interviews with executive MBA alumni at the University of Arkansas, polled across twenty-nine potential barriers, put lack of understanding first. Not of the technology, and not of the use cases: of the gap between a pilot that proves a concept and the thing working at scale across a supply chain. After that, in order, came integration into existing processes, the benefits, the implementation cost, uncertainty about return, and the technical limits. The thesis notes that the sample size is not stated, which is the right thing to note.

The rest of the barriers are about other people. Collaboration, because a party that joins may be giving information to a competitor or losing its role in the chain altogether. Standardisation and interoperability, because terminology, measurement and systems differ between the firms who would have to share. Bureaucracies, because supply chains cross borders and not every agency wants that much transparency. Competitive instinct, because information asymmetry is somebody’s advantage and they know it.

And two that are properties of the technology rather than of the people. Immutability, which protects a correct record and preserves a wrong one just as permanently. And security, where the thesis is bluntest: once an exploit is found there is no countermeasure fast enough, and a contract written with every protection its author could think of is still exposed to somebody more inventive.

What it concludes

Not that blockchain fixes invoicing. That specific, identified cases exist where it does, and that a company should find its own through pilots and simulations before adopting anything, because that is the only way to learn where the technology is weak in its particular context.

Smaller companies may simply not have the investment required, and the thesis says so rather than pretending otherwise. It also warns against the thing that was happening while it was being written: adoption driven by the word rather than by a business case. Organisations that do adopt should customise the contracts to their own business rules, not inherit someone else’s.

The honest gap, named in the conclusion: there is no profile that safely examines a company’s order-to-cash and procure-to-pay processes before and after adoption. Until somebody builds one across several organisations, using qualitative and quantitative methods together, the case rests on industry reporting. The next step the thesis points at is blockchain with the internet of things.
